RF & Antennas · Engineering Case Study

2.45 GHz Free-Space RF Propagation

Technical analysis, original simulation results and engineering decisions.

ESIEE Paris · Project team: Kévin Kao · Rania Chettar · Anaïs Sallard

This simulation study investigated how received RF power depends on load matching, antenna orientation and separation. The initial model used two resonant dipoles at 2.45 GHz, with a separation of one metre and a resistive load at the receiver.

Impedance matching

The power delivered to the load depends on the complex receiving-antenna impedance. For a purely resistive approximation, maximum power transfer occurs when the load resistance matches the antenna’s source resistance; in the general AC case, conjugate matching is required.

Conjugate matching
\(Z_{\mathrm{L}}=Z_{\mathrm{ant}}^{*}\)

Load sweeps were used to compare received power at different termination resistances. Dipole orientation was also varied to explore polarization mismatch: rotating the receiving antenna away from the transmitter’s polarization reduces the coupled field component.

Free-space path loss

The Friis transmission equation predicts received power in the far field, under matched polarization and impedance assumptions:

Friis transmission equation
\(P_{\mathrm{r}}=P_{\mathrm{t}}G_{\mathrm{t}}G_{\mathrm{r}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2\)

At fixed frequency and antenna gains, power decreases approximately as the inverse square of distance. In logarithmic units, doubling distance introduces roughly 6 dB additional free-space path loss.

Comparison of antennas

The report then compared dipole and directional-antenna geometries. The resulting curves emphasize the distinction between radiated power, antenna directivity, impedance matching and actual received power. The Friis law is a far-field reference model, not a universal near-field formula.
