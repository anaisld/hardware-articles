Understanding Free-Space RF Propagation: Dipoles, Polarization and Antenna Directivity

A 2.45 GHz antenna-modeling study exploring impedance matching, orientation and the inverse-square law with 4NEC2 simulations.

How much of the power radiated by one antenna reaches another? The answer depends on more than the transmitter: the receiving load, antenna orientation, propagation distance and radiation pattern all influence the result. This laboratory investigation used 4NEC2 to study a pair of resonant dipoles and compare them with a more directive antenna arrangement.

Engineering objective

Maximize power transfer to a receiving antenna, investigate polarization mismatch, validate the free-space distance law and quantify the benefit of a more directive geometry.

Building the dipole-to-dipole link

Two identical dipoles were modeled at 2,450 MHz, with a conductor length of approximately 0.46 wavelength. One acted as a transmitter; the other was terminated by a resistive load representing the receiving electronics. The initial separation was 1 m, and the simulation specified a transmitter excitation power of 1 MW. This high value is a modeling input, not a claim about a physical transmitter.

Wavelength in free space
λ = c / f

At f = 2.45 GHz, the free-space wavelength is approximately 0.122 m, giving L ≈ 0.46λ ≈ 56 mm for the modeled dipole.

Matching the receiver load

The first parameter sweep changed the receiving load through 10, 50, 70, 100 and 250 Ω. The report identifies 70 Ω as the resistance yielding the highest received power in the simulated configurations, consistent with the approximate resistive input impedance of a resonant half-wave dipole.

Maximum power transfer principle
ZL = Zsource*

For a source with impedance Zsource, the load receives maximum available power when it is the complex conjugate of that impedance. If the reactance is negligible, this becomes approximately RL = Rsource.

A useful distinction: impedance matching maximizes power transfer to the load; it is not, by itself, proof that the two antennas are in resonance. The dipoles were designed to be resonant near the operating frequency.

Why antenna orientation matters

Keeping the 70 Ω load, the receiving dipole was rotated through 0°, 25°, 50°, 75° and 90°. The reported received power decreased as the two dipoles became progressively misaligned. The radiation-pattern simulation provides a complementary view of the antenna's directional response.

Ideal polarization mismatch
Pr(θ) / Pr(0) = cos2(θ)

For two linearly polarized antennas with otherwise unchanged geometry, an angular polarization mismatch θ introduces a power factor of cos²θ. In practice, rotating a dipole may also change its alignment with the radiation pattern, so the full 4NEC2 result need not be explained by polarization alone.

Distance dependence: the dipole case

Next, the receiving dipole was moved from 1 m to 160 m. The reported power dropped from 252 W at 1 m to approximately 0.01 W at 160 m. Doubling the distance from 1 m to 2 m reduced the reported received power from 252 W to 63.1 W — almost exactly a factor of four.

Friis transmission equation — ideal far field
Pr = Pt Gt Gr (λ / 4πR)2

Pt is transmitted power, Gt and Gr are antenna gains, λ is wavelength and R is separation. For fixed frequency and antenna gains, Pr ∝ 1/R².

Expressing received power in decibel-milliwatts makes the same relationship easier to inspect on a logarithmic distance axis.

Link budget and logarithmic slope
Pr,dBm = 10 log10(Pr,W / 0.001)
Pr,dBm = C − 20 log10(R / 1 m)

Here, C groups the fixed link parameters for a given geometry. Increasing distance by a factor of ten therefore lowers received power by approximately 20 dB in the ideal free-space far field.

The reported 4NEC2 trend follows the expected −20 dB per decade slope, supporting the inverse-square propagation model.

What changes with directive antennas?

The final comparison replaced the dipole-only arrangement with simple directive antennas incorporating a ground plane. The report describes placing the radiating element about λ/4 above the ground plane to exploit reflected fields and direct more energy into useful directions.

With this geometry, the reported received power was 8,034 W at 1 m and 0.34 W at 160 m. The distance-dependent slope remained close to −20 dB/decade, while the entire received-power curve shifted upward relative to the dipole case.

Comparing link gain at 1 m
ΔPdB = 10 log10(8034 / 252)
ΔPdB ≈ 15.0 dB

The report describes a roughly 15 dB advantage in received power for the directive configuration. This is a link-level improvement for the modeled geometries; the available data do not independently isolate transmitter gain, receiver gain and reflection contributions.

Engineering interpretation

The simulation highlights three distinct design levers. Impedance matching determines how efficiently the received signal reaches the load. Polarization and radiation pattern determine how effectively the antennas couple in a given orientation. Directivity can improve received power at a target location, but does not remove free-space spreading loss.

The results are numerical simulations of idealized antenna configurations, not over-the-air measurements. Real links additionally involve cable and connector losses, multipath, obstacles, regulatory power limits and receiver sensitivity.

Engineering takeaway

A good RF link is not designed by increasing transmit power alone. Matching, polarization, antenna geometry and a quantified link budget are essential to predicting the power actually delivered to a receiver.
