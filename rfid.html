How a Contactless Card Talks: Modeling a 13.56 MHz RFID Link

From resonant antennas and magnetic coupling to passive power harvesting and recovering a weak RF reply — an analog design study using PSpice.

A contactless transport card has no battery, yet it can respond to a reader in a fraction of a second. The engineering challenge is that the same magnetic field must deliver energy to the card and carry information in both directions. We investigated the physical layer of a 13.56 MHz high-frequency RFID system through AC and transient circuit simulations.

Engineering objective

Tune a resonant reader, model its coupling to a passive tag, recover a usable DC supply, and determine how analog filtering affects the detection of load-modulated data.

Tuning the reader: resonance and impedance

The first task was to adjust the reader antenna network around the 13.56 MHz carrier. A PSpice frequency sweep identified a 10 pF tuning parameter for the tested circuit. Before comparing waveforms, we estimated resonance from the simplified equivalent LC network.

Thomson resonance formula · LC circuit
f0 = 1 / (2π√(LC))

Using L = 900 nH and Ceq = 156 pF:

f0 ≈ 1 / (2π√(900 nH × 156 pF)) ≈ 13.43 MHz
The equivalent capacitance belongs to the simplified network; it is not the adjustable 10 pF component alone.

The input impedance was estimated near 50 Ω at the operating point. The modeled antenna quality factor was approximately 8.8: resonance improves voltage build-up, but excessive selectivity can suppress the sidebands needed for communication.

Ohm’s law · Input impedance
|Zin| = |Vin| / |Iin|
|Zin| ≈ 3.05 V / 60.9 mA ≈ 50.1 Ω
This is a magnitude estimate; full RF matching also requires the impedance phase.

Coupling two antennas without a wire

The reader and tag are coupled inductors. We varied the tag inductance between 0.9 and 1.4 µH and compared weak (1%) with strong (50%) magnetic coupling. At weak coupling, the tag only slightly perturbed the reader response. At strong coupling, the sweep developed two resonant peaks — a signature of coupled-resonator mode splitting.

Mutual inductance · Magnetically coupled coils
M = k√(LreaderLtag)

The coupling coefficient k describes how strongly the two coils exchange magnetic flux. It changes with distance, orientation and geometry.

Powering a passive tag

On the tag side, the induced RF voltage was rectified and filtered. In the simulated startup transient, the DC supply rose toward 4.3 V, demonstrating the operation of the modeled energy-harvesting path. Whether that supply can run a real chip also depends on its current draw and voltage limits.

Recovering the tag’s reply

Instead of transmitting with a separate power amplifier, the tag changes its electrical load. This load modulation produces a small variation reflected back into the reader antenna. The receiver model used I/Q synchronous detection, low-pass filtering, envelope processing and thresholding to reconstruct the simulated data pattern.

The important difficulty is not simply detecting a voltage: the receiver must distinguish a weak modulation from the much larger RF carrier while preserving the timing of the data.

Finding the right filter under weak coupling

At 2% coupling, varying the reader-coil resistance showed a trade-off between signal amplitude and bandwidth. Around 5 Ω gave the clearest qualitative recovery in this simulation; this is not a universal design value.

We then adjusted the envelope detector. A small RC time constant follows transitions quickly but allows more ripple; a larger one smooths the signal at the cost of delay. The tested weak-coupling case favored 22 kΩ and 220 pF.

RC time-constant law · Envelope filtering
τ = RC
τ = 22 kΩ × 220 pF = 4.84 µs

This value was a qualitative compromise between ripple rejection and response time for the simulated receiver.

Engineering takeaway

The main lesson is a system-level one: antenna tuning, magnetic coupling, harvested power, receiver bandwidth and filter timing cannot be optimized independently. A change that strengthens the carrier can make its modulation harder to recover.

Scope of the study

This was a PSpice physical-layer simulation inspired by HF RFID systems. It did not read an actual Navigo card, extract a real identifier or establish full ISO/IEC 14443 compliance.
