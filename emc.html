Electromagnetic Compatibility: Crosstalk & Signal Integrity

Technical analysis, original simulation results and engineering decisions.

ESIEE Paris · Project team: Kévin Kao · Rania Chettar · Anaïs Sallard

This laboratory examined two closely related high-speed electronics problems: unwanted coupling between neighboring conductors and waveform distortion along interconnects. The experiments studied transient crosstalk before moving to signal-integrity simulations.

Why adjacent traces couple

Changing voltage on one conductor produces displacement current through mutual capacitance, while changing current creates magnetic coupling through mutual inductance. Together, these mechanisms induce unwanted signals on a neighboring line. The coupling depends on geometry, return-current paths, edge speed and termination.

Reflections and termination

A transmission line is characterized by its impedance and propagation delay. At a load discontinuity, the voltage reflection coefficient is:

Load reflection coefficient
\(\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{L}}-Z_0}{Z_{\mathrm{L}}+Z_0}\)

When the termination matches the characteristic impedance, the ideal reflection coefficient is zero. In practice, transitions, connectors and source impedance can also affect the observed waveform.

Near-end and far-end crosstalk

The report separated backward-traveling (near-end) and forward-traveling (far-end) interference and varied signal rise time and conductor arrangement. Faster edges generally increase the importance of distributed electromagnetic effects. Ground conductors can alter coupling, but their effectiveness depends on their placement and continuity.

Engineering lesson

EMC and signal integrity are not independent afterthoughts: layout geometry, controlled impedance, return paths and realistic edge rates should be considered during circuit and PCB design rather than only after a failed test.
